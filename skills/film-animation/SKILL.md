# Film & Animation Craft

**Animation-first, live-action-informed.** When the work is 3D/CG, motion graphics, or
any frame-by-frame construction, the 12 principles of animation (Lasseter) are the spine.
The live-action craft in here — lighting, staging, cutting, performance — is the depth
layer that makes animation read as intentional rather than merely correct.

## When to load this skill

Load it when you are about to make or judge decisions about moving images:

- Planning a shot list, coverage plan, or camera setup
- Storyboarding a script, explainer, or sequence
- Blocking/staging a scene (where actors/camera/objects go and move)
- Designing a lighting setup or a mood
- Cutting a sequence — where to cut, what to cut to, what to leave out
- Timing motion, a cut, a hold, or an action
- Directing or animating a performance
- Designing a shot that cannot be filmed practically (effect, miniature, comp)
- Reviewing a rendered video for craft quality

Don't load it for: pure colour-grading maths, audio mixing levels, codec/export settings,
or asset-library tasks. This is about *intent and staging*, not bitrate.

## How to use this skill

Two modes of content below:

1. **Decision trees (§1)** — start here. Find your task, follow the branch. Each step
   names the sources it draws on.
2. **Glossary (§2)** — the vocabulary, attributed to its source, for when you need to
   reason about *why* rather than *what next*.

Then read **§3 When to break the default** before you commit a choice. Craft rules in
this corpus are defaults with explicit licences to break them, and knowing which side of
a tension you're on is the difference between a rule-follower and a filmmaker.

**Cite sources, not page numbers.** Every report in the corpus flagged page numbers as
unverified (most of these books are print/lending-restricted). Attribute an idea to its
author and work — "Murch's Rule of Six", "Alton's mystery lighting" — never to a page.

## 0. The craft order

Craft decisions cascade. Fix them in this order or you'll re-litigate the wrong problem.

```
story / through-line          ← Mamet, Pudovkin, Reisz
   ↓
staging / blocking            ← Katz, Arijon, Mascelli
   ↓
camera placement & coverage   ← Mascelli, Arijon, Kenworthy, Begleiter
   ↓
lighting                      ← Alton, Millerson, Almendros, Lowell
   ↓
performance                   ← Caine, Pudovkin, Mamet
   ↓
edit / montage                ← Murch, Eisenstein, Pudovkin, Reisz & Millar
   ↓
pacing & sound                ← Murch, Eisenstein, Bordwell
```

Two inversions that are *not* mistakes: lighting is often decided *before* coverage on a
schedule (Millerson: light the set before the actors arrive; Bergery/Burum same), and a
cut's rhythm may be discovered in the edit and sent back up to reshoot coverage
(Reisz & Millar on building artificial rhythm in the cutting room).

## 1. Decision trees

### 1.1 Staging a scene (blocking)

*Sources: Katz, Arijon, Mascelli, Begleiter, Kenworthy, Bordwell.*

1. **Name the through-line.** What must the audience understand or feel by the end of
   this scene? Every staging choice serves that or goes (Mamet, Katz).
2. **Answer Katz's two gating questions first** — they constrain everything else:
   - Where is the camera?
   - How do the actors move within that frame?
3. **Choose the point of view.** Katz: POV is the single most significant determinant of
   camera placement. Whose experience is this? Inside the action, or outside observing it
   (the *dramatic circle of action*)?
4. **Decide the staging philosophy:**
   - *Realism* — stage the action, then find the camera that photographs it naturally.
   - *Expressionism* — stage the action **for** the camera; the camera's position is part
     of the meaning.
   Most commercial work mixes both. Name which one each beat is.
5. **Apply the line of interest (Mascelli's action axis / Arijon's triangle principle).**
   All cameras stay on one side of the line between the actors. Set the master shot on
   that side, then derive every other setup from it.
6. **Stage for depth (Almendros, Lowell, Katz).** Manufacture depth: separate planes
   (foreground / midground / background), overlap, and a change in scale. Flat staging is
   a choice, not a default.
7. **Count the players** and use Arijon's staging patterns: 2-player and 3-player dialogue
   have canonical placement patterns; 4+ needs a different grouping logic.
8. **Budget the moves.** Arijon's twenty rules for camera movement reduce to one test:
   *is there solid dramatic motivation?* If no, lock off (Kenworthy: never move for its
   own sake). Katz: every staging choice costs shooting time.
9. **Give actors reasons, not marks** (Katz, via Sayles). Blocking that only positions
   bodies reads as choreography; blocking that grows from what a character wants reads as
   behaviour.

### 1.2 Coverage and shot selection

*Sources: Mamet, Mascelli, Arijon, Kenworthy, Begleiter, Bordwell.*

1. **Fix the unit of work.** Mamet: film → scene → beat → **shot** (the smallest unit).
   Build scenes from single-sentence beats.
2. **Shoot the master first** (Mascelli's master-scene technique) — it establishes the
   line, the geography, and the fallback. Then cover.
3. **Choose the coverage strategy:**
   - *Master-scene* — one long take covers the scene; pickups only where needed.
   - *Triple-take* (Mascelli)/coverage — master + mediums + close-ups, protection.
   - *Sequence shot* — no cuts; staging does the work (Katz: complementary to coverage,
     not a replacement).
   - *Segment shooting* (Bordwell) — shoot one setup at a time, all its coverage, before
     moving the camera. Fast and coherent; not coverage in the Hollywood sense.
4. **Never cut for the sake of coverage.** Mamet: refuse to "follow the protagonist",
   refuse the gratuitous Steadicam; tell the story in cuts. Kenworthy: 100 setups to make
   dialogue not dull — but only if each one *means* something.
5. **Protect the geography.** Mascelli: subject size, subject angle, camera height — three
   variables per shot; don't move more than one arbitrarily between shots that should
   match.
6. **Cut only across sufficient change.** Reisz & Millar: a size/angle change big enough to
   justify the cut. Too small = jarring; too large = disorienting.
7. **Watch the eyeline.** Arijon's opposed glances; Mascelli's matching of position,
   movement and look between consecutive shots.
8. **Check the frame before you shoot** (Weston's previsualization): know the composition
   you want before the camera rolls; the image is composed, not cropped.
9. **Storyboard by risk, not taste** (Begleiter). Board what is uncertain: action, crowds,
   complex moves, VFX, montage, openers — in editorial sequence, not shooting order.

### 1.3 Lighting a mood

*Sources: Alton, Millerson, Almendros, Lowell, Weston, Bergery/Burum.*

1. **Start from intention, not instruments.** Alton: light serves mood, never exposure.
   Lowell: visibility is the *minimum* aim, not the goal. Ask what the scene should feel
   like before asking what lamp.
2. **Set the three universal functions** (Bergery/Burum): cast a shadow, create separation,
   add fill. If a lamp does none of these, question it.
3. **Choose quality before quantity.** Lowell: hard vs soft is physics — source size and
   distance. Decide *quality* first; footcandles are subordinate.
4. **Decide the contrast ratio** — the ratio between key and fill is a designed number, not
   a by-product (Millerson). Lowell: fill is surgical; over-fill produces
   **Flat-Light Syndrome**.
5. **Decide the angle.** Lowell: angle outranks all else for mood and modelling. Millerson:
   front / side / edge / back, and lamp height.
6. **Choose the source philosophy:**
   | Approach | Method | Champion |
   |---|---|---|
   | Mystery / low-key | darkness is a positive element; shadows are equipment | Alton |
   | Classic studio three-point | key + fill + back, balanced | Millerson |
   | Available / natural | find the light, subtract what isn't real; "de-illuminate" | Almendros |
   | Motivated minimal | light the source you can justify; depth over brightness | Lowell |
7. **Manufacture depth.** Alton: dark foreground → light background. Lowell: separate
   planes. Weston: light is *material and subject*, not just illumination.
8. **Motivate every source.** If the audience can't believe the light, it doesn't matter
   how beautiful it is (Almendros, Lowell, Millerson).
9. **Protect continuity.** Millerson: visual continuity of light across discontinuous
   shooting. Almendros: light continuity across the cut — the "two suns" problem.
10. **Previsualize.** Alton and Weston both: know the finished image before you light or
    expose. (Almendros: expose for the face, kill the postcard.)
11. **Let painters inform, not dictate** (Almendros): reference, don't copy.

### 1.4 Cutting a sequence

*Sources: Murch, Reisz & Millar, Pudovkin, Eisenstein, Mamet, Mascelli, Bordwell.*

1. **Set the emotion.** Murch's Rule of Six governs every cut, in priority order:
   **emotion 51 · story 23 · rhythm 10 · eye-trace 7 · 2-D plane of screen 5 · 3-D space
   of action 4.** When two criteria conflict, sacrifice from the bottom up. An
   emotionally right cut that breaks the axis beats a geometrically perfect one that
   doesn't.
2. **Find the blink.** Murch: the cut lands where the audience would blink — at the end of
   a thought. Rhythm of thought, not rhythm of frames.
3. **Cut out the bad bits.** Murch: editing is mostly subtraction. Mamet: get in late, get
   out early. Mascelli: the diamond metaphor — the film is improved by what is removed.
4. **Choose the montage theory** your sequence needs:
   | Theory | Mechanism | Source |
   |---|---|---|
   | Linkage / brickwork | shots add up (A+B→AB); guide attention | Pudovkin |
   | Collision | shots collide (A+B→C); meaning is a product | Eisenstein |
   | Coherence + drama | continuity serves the flow of ideas | Reisz & Millar |
   | Juxtaposition of uninflected images | minimum-information shots, let the cut speak | Mamet |
   Most work is linkage; use collision deliberately for argument or shock.
5. **Match across the cut** (Reisz & Millar): consecutive actions matched; cut *with* the
   movement, not against it; match tone; sound bridge over the cut.
6. **Preserve screen direction** unless the discontinuity is intentional (Reisz & Millar,
   Mascelli, Arijon). A crossed line is a meaning, not an accident.
7. **Protect the performance.** Reisz & Millar: choose the take where the performance
   lives, then make the rhythm work around it. Rhythm may be manufactured in the cut room
   — but never at the cost of the actor.
8. **Build rhythm** with Eisenstein's ladder: metric → rhythmic → tonal → overtonal →
   intellectual. Most sequences need metric + rhythmic; reach for tonal/overtonal when
   the sequence must carry feeling, not just information.
9. **Use sound as an axis** (Murch, Eisenstein's vertical montage, Pudovkin's
   asynchronism): sound and image can contradict productively. Do not simply illustrate.
10. **Audience-ombudsman test** (Murch): you are the first audience. If you're confused
    about *what happens next*, so are they. If you're bored, cut.
11. **Amplify with the cut** (Bordwell): a shot can be cut to *increase* physical or
    emotional charge — expressive amplification, not mere continuity.

### 1.5 Timing motion (animation and motion graphics)

*Sources: Lasseter (via Thomas & Johnston), Rickitt (for filmed motion), Murch (for
cut rhythm).*

1. **Anticipate before you act.** The audience reads intention from preparation. Lead with
   the eyes and head, then the body (Lasseter).
2. **Squash and stretch, volume-constant.** Deformation sells weight and material — rigid
   objects barely, soft ones hugely. Preserve volume unless the character is a cartoon
   whose design permits otherwise.
3. **Timing is the personality.** Spacing (the distance between keys) defines weight, size
   and character. Two identical poses with different spacing are two different characters.
4. **Slow in / slow out — but author it.** Don't accept easing defaults. The ease *is* the
   performance.
5. **Arcs.** Almost nothing moves in a straight line — put an arc on every path of action,
   however subtle.
6. **Follow-through and overlapping action.** Nothing stops at once. Parts lag, drag and
   settle. In CG this becomes the **moving hold** — the pose stays "alive" rather than
   freezing.
7. **Pose-to-pose vs straight-ahead.** For a pose-driven, readable result, key down the rig
   hierarchy (root → spine → limbs → extremities). For naturalistic motion, work
   straight-ahead. CG work is usually pose-to-pose, hierarchical.
8. **Secondary action subordinately.** It enriches but must never compete with the primary
   action for attention.
9. **Exaggerate the essence, matched to design.** Push the idea past literal, but stay
   inside the character's style. (Lasseter's critique of raw mocap: motion without
   exaggeration reads as uncanny, not alive.)
10. **Solid drawing.** Three-dimensionality, weight, balance, and avoiding "twins"
    (mirrored, dead-symmetrical poses). Keep two views of the character alive at once.
11. **Appeal.** The goal of everything above. Appeal is charisma, not prettiness — a
    character an audience wants to watch.
12. **Staging (again).** One unmistakable idea at a time — this principle is shared with
    live-action staging (§1.1) and is the hinge between the two traditions.

### 1.6 Directing / animating performance

*Sources: Caine, Pudovkin, Mamet, Katz, Kenworthy.*

1. **Screen acting is a reaction medium** (Caine). Acting is listening and reacting, not
   delivering. Animate the reaction, not just the line.
2. **Stillness and the eyes.** Caine: don't blink, pick one eye and hold it. On screen the
   smallest change carries. **Thought recognition** — the reaction lands on the key word.
3. **Prepare cold to stay spontaneous** (Caine). Over-rehearsal kills the first-take
   aliveness; know your job, then let it be fresh.
4. **Respect the technical frame** (Caine): marks, lens approach, repeatability across
   master/medium/close-up. In animation, this is the equivalent of consistent staging and
   eyeline across shots.
5. **Give simple physical action, not feelings** (Mamet). Direct what the character *does*;
   the emotion is the audience's inference.
6. **The performer is material shaped by the director** (Pudovkin) — his most contestable
   idea. Treat it as one pole: the other is Caine's actor-as-collaborator. Meet in the
   middle: shape the performance, don't just record it (Kenworthy: shooting performance is
   inseparable from directing it).
7. **Generosity, not competition** (Caine). Ensemble performance reads better than one
   actor winning the frame. In animation: the secondary character's reaction must be
   played, not dead.

### 1.7 Designing an impossible shot (effects, miniatures, comp)

*Sources: Rickitt, Millerson, Begleiter.*

1. **Understand that the frame is the illusion** (Rickitt). At 24 fps (or any frame rate)
   you are combining images filmed at different times and places. Everything follows.
2. **Match geometry.** Perspective, nodal point, forced perspective. If the geometry
   doesn't match, nothing else can save it.
3. **Make camera motion reproducible.** Locked-off, or motion-control. A hand-held move
   cannot be repeated across elements.
4. **Match photographic qualities** (Rickitt): lens, light, contrast, spill, gate weave.
   The comp fails on the *mismatched* qualities, not the missing ones.
5. **Be scale-honest.** No detail on distant objects; some things (water, fire, atmosphere)
   don't scale — intercut or cheat instead.
6. **Shoot the model like a live-action unit.** Restrained camera. Composition is
   camouflage: build the **pyramid of believability** — the reveal lands where the audience
   is already looking.
7. **Plan before shooting** (Begleiter + Rickitt): animatics, storyboards, clean plates.
   The effect is decided in preproduction, not in post.
8. **Performance is the proof.** A convincingly animated creature is a performance problem
   before it is a rendering problem (Rickitt).
9. **Practical and digital are one continuum** (Rickitt). Choose per shot on grounds of
   believability and schedule, not ideology.

### 1.8 Boarding it (script → images)

*Sources: Begleiter, Katz, Mascelli.*

1. **The board is a visual script, not an illustration** (Begleiter). It *translates*; it
   doesn't decorate.
2. **Use all three tiers:** text, overhead diagram, image. The **overhead diagram**
   (plan view) is often the most powerful document — it communicates blocking in a way
   neither prose nor a single frame can.
3. **Board in editorial sequence**, not shooting order. You are designing the cut.
4. **Choose the board type by purpose:** editorial draft (exploratory), key frame
   (the one that defines the look), production illustration (polished, for the client),
   commercial comp (pitch).
5. **Board by logistical risk** (see §1.2 step 9).
6. **Attend to the frame:** aspect ratio, and an **extended frame** around the delivery
   frame so camera movement has somewhere to go.
7. **Compose for narrative**, and use figure notation and perspective so the board is
   legible to the crew (Begleiter).

## 2. Principle glossary (attributed)

### Staging & blocking
- **Line of interest / action axis** — the imaginary line between subjects; keep the camera
  on one side. *(Mascelli; Arijon's "triangle principle")*
- **Dramatic circle of action** — whether the camera sits inside or outside the action.
  *(Katz)*
- **Staging as a plan, not a series of decisions** — commit to a pattern of movement.
  *(Katz)*
- **Three staging considerations** — narrative, dramatic, pictorial. *(Katz)*
- **Dramatic emphasis = shot size + frame placement, governed by contrast.** *(Katz)*
- **Reasons, not marks** — blocking derived from character intention. *(Katz)*
- **Frame-centred staging** — stage for what is *in* the frame, aggressively.
  *(Bordwell)*

### Composition & the camera
- **The three variables** — subject size, subject angle, camera height. *(Mascelli)*
- **Master-scene vs triple-take** — one long take vs master + coverage. *(Mascelli,
  terms he coined)*
- **Cut in on the motive** — cut when the audience has a reason to look. *(Mascelli)*
- **Composition in space *and* time** — a shot is arranged across duration, not just
  across the frame. *(Mascelli)*
- **The strongest way of seeing** — composition is not a rulebook, it is the clearest
  possible arrangement. *(Weston)*
- **Previsualization** — know the finished frame before you expose it. *(Weston,
  Alton)*
- **Lens choice is emotional control.** *(Kenworthy)*

### Lighting
- **Mystery lighting** — darkness as a positive element; light reveals selectively.
  *(Alton)*
- **Light serves mood, not exposure.** *(Alton)*
- **Shadows are equipment** — subtracting light is a tool, not a compromise. *(Alton)*
- **Full grey scale** — a full tonal range; "two intensities of light are wearisome."
  *(Alton)*
- **Symphonic construction** — light the whole film, not the shot. *(Alton)*
- **Three-point lighting** — key, fill, back, plus balance. *(Millerson)*
- **Shadow density defines form.** *(Millerson)*
- **Light as attention-direction** — lighting tells the eye where to look. *(Millerson)*
- **"Look and light" vs "plot and light"** — improvised vs planned lighting methods.
  *(Millerson)*
- **Contrast as a designed ratio.** *(Millerson, Lowell)*
- **Flat-Light Syndrome** — over-filled light that kills form. *(Lowell)*
- **Quality is physics** — source size and distance determine hard/soft. *(Lowell)*
- **"Light and depth"** — light's job is to create depth and meaning. *(Lowell)*
- **"De-illuminating"** — realism achieved by subtracting light. *(Almendros)*
- **Expose for the face; kill the postcard.** *(Almendros)*
- **Two suns** — the continuity problem of matching light across cuts. *(Almendros)*
- **Three universal lighting functions** — cast a shadow, create separation, add fill.
  *(Bergery / Burum)*

### Editing & montage
- **Rule of Six** — emotion 51 · story 23 · rhythm 10 · eye-trace 7 · 2-D plane 5 ·
  3-D space 4; sacrifice bottom-up. *(Murch)*
- **The blink** — cut where the audience would blink, at the end of a thought. *(Murch)*
- **Dream state** — film is closer to dreaming than to seeing; discontinuity is native.
  *(Murch)*
- **Audience ombudsman** — the editor represents the audience, not the director.
  *(Murch)*
- **Montage as collision** — A+B→C; meaning is a *product*, not a sum. *(Eisenstein)*
- **Metric / rhythmic / tonal / overtonal / intellectual montage** — the ladder of
  editing intensity. *(Eisenstein)*
- **Vertical montage** — sound and image as simultaneous contrapuntal tracks.
  *(Eisenstein)*
- **Montage of attractions** — agitational juxtaposition of powerful moments.
  *(Eisenstein)*
- **Linkage / brickwork** — shots add up to a guided whole. *(Pudovkin)*
- **The five relational methods** — contrast, parallelism, symbolism, simultaneity,
  leit-motif. *(Pudovkin)*
- **Editing is juxtaposition** — same shots, reordered, make new meaning (Kuleshov).
  *(Pudovkin, Eisenstein, Reisz & Millar)*
- **The uninflected image** — minimum-information shots; the cut supplies the meaning.
  *(Mamet)*
- **Cut with the movement, not against it.** *(Reisz & Millar)*
- **Flow of ideas over mechanical smoothness.** *(Reisz & Millar)*
- **Selection is the prime creative act.** *(Reisz & Millar)*

### Pacing & rhythm
- **Pause–burst–pause** — stasis → sustained movement → stasis; the action-set rhythm.
  *(Bordwell)*
- **Expressive amplification** — filmic handling magnifies charge rather than merely
  recording it. *(Bordwell)*
- **Get in late, get out early.** *(Mamet)*
- **Always do the least interesting thing** — refuse the decorative shot. *(Mamet)*
- **Rhythm of thought** — cut at the pace of thinking, not of frames. *(Murch)*

### Animation & timing
- **Squash & stretch · anticipation · staging · straight-ahead vs pose-to-pose ·
  follow-through & overlapping action · slow in/slow out · arcs · secondary action ·
  timing · exaggeration · solid drawing · appeal** — the 12 principles. *(Lasseter, after
  Thomas & Johnston)*
- **Moving hold** — the CG realisation of follow-through: a pose that stays alive.
  *(Lasseter)*
- **Key down the hierarchy** — root → spine → limbs → extremities for pose-to-pose CG.
  *(Lasseter)*
- **Exaggerate the essence, matched to design.** *(Lasseter)*

### Performance
- **Stage vs screen: scalpel vs laser.** *(Caine)*
- **Don't blink; pick one eye and hold it.** *(Caine)*
- **Acting is reacting and listening.** *(Caine)*
- **Thought recognition** — land the reaction on the key word. *(Caine)*
- **Prepare cold for spontaneity.** *(Caine)*
- **The actor as material** — anti-theatrical, shaped by the director. *(Pudovkin;
  "Types Instead of Actors")*
- **Give physical action, not feelings.** *(Mamet)*

### Story & structure
- **Through-line / super-objective** governs every shot. *(Mamet)*
- **The shot is the smallest unit; the scene is the director's unit.** *(Mamet)*
- **Meaning is made between shots.** *(Mamet, Eisenstein, Pudovkin)*
- **Legibility first** — action must be instantly readable. *(Bordwell)*
- **Genre engines** — kung fu, wuxia, heroic bloodshed; each genre has its own vocabulary.
  *(Bordwell)*
- **Craft under constraint** — constraint is enabling, not a defect. *(Bordwell)*

### Effects
- **The frame is the illusion.** *(Rickitt)*
- **Matched geometry** — perspective, nodal point, forced perspective. *(Rickitt)*
- **Pyramid of believability** — composition as camouflage for the effect. *(Rickitt)*
- **Some things don't scale** — water, fire, atmosphere. *(Rickitt)*
- **Performance is the proof.** *(Rickitt)*

## 3. When to break the default

These are the tensions in the corpus. Know which side you're on; don't split the
difference by accident.

| Tension | Default | Break it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Uninflected shots (Mamet) vs expressive frame (Alton, Almendros)** | Shoot plain, let the cut make meaning | The image itself must carry emotion — then compose and light for it |
| **Linkage (Pudovkin) vs collision (Eisenstein)** | Shots add up, guide attention | You need argument, shock, or a leap the audience must make themselves |
| **Continuity (Reisz & Millar, Mascelli) vs discontinuity (Murch, Eisenstein)** | Preserve the line and the flow of ideas | Discontinuity *is* the meaning, or emotion outranks geometry (Murch's 51%) |
| **Rules (Mascelli, Arijon) vs eye (Weston, Lowell, Mamet)** | Apply the grammar | You can see the better shot; then take it and be accountable for it |
| **Restraint (Rickitt, Bordwell) vs amplification (Bordwell)** | Hold the camera, keep it legible | The sequence must intensify — then cut and move for charge |

Lowell's own closing advice is the meta-rule: **learn the rules so well you can forget
them.** Rules exist to be exceeded by someone who knows why they exist.

## 4. Pre-flight checklists

**Shot / coverage pass**
- [ ] Through-line named for every scene
- [ ] Line of interest set; all cameras on one side
- [ ] Master covered first; pickups only where the cut needs them
- [ ] Every cut justified by a change in size, angle, or information
- [ ] Eyelines and screen direction consistent
- [ ] Nothing in frame that the cut could say better

**Lighting pass**
- [ ] Intention/mood named before instruments chosen
- [ ] Three functions present: shadow, separation, fill
- [ ] Quality (hard/soft) and angle chosen deliberately
- [ ] Contrast ratio is a decision, not a by-product
- [ ] Depth manufactured: planes separated, form modelled
- [ ] Every source motivated and believable
- [ ] Light continuity across planned cuts

**Edit pass**
- [ ] Emotion checked first; lower criteria sacrificed before it
- [ ] Blink/thought rhythm respected, not frame rhythm
- [ ] Cuts removed everything that could be removed
- [ ] Performance protected where it lives
- [ ] Sound bridges and contradictions used, not just illustrated
- [ ] A first-time viewer knows *what happens next* at every cut

**Animation pass**
- [ ] Anticipation before every significant action
- [ ] Volume preserved through squash/stretch
- [ ] Easing authored, not defaulted
- [ ] Arcs on all paths of action
- [ ] Overlap/follow-through on everything that stops
- [ ] Secondary action subordinate
- [ ] Exaggeration matched to character design
- [ ] Poses avoid "twins"; two views alive
- [ ] Staging reads one idea at a time

## 6. Provenance & caveats

- **How this was built.** 19 parallel research passes (2026-10-10), each producing a
  structured report in a shared corpus, then synthesized here. The full research
  corpus (~790 KB) and its evidence trail back the provenance checks below.
- **Page numbers are unreliable.** Reports consistently flagged page numbers as
  unverified — most of these texts are print- or lending-restricted. Attribute ideas to
  author + work, never to a page. Any quote you intend to publish should be checked
  against a physical or licensed copy first.
- **Five titles were corrected.** Four works as originally listed do not exist
  (see §5, items 15–18). Reisz & Millar was commissioned by the **British Film Academy**,
  not the BFI. Katz's "visual plane" is not a Katz term.
- **Not verified as Murch:** the phrase "sound as the nervous system of film" turned up
  attributed to another craftsman and is deliberately excluded.
- **One quote deliberately excluded:** a widely-circulated "be nice on the way up" line
  attributed to Caine could not be verified and is not used.
- **Live-action craft, animation lens.** Where the corpus disagrees (collision vs linkage,
  rules vs eye), both are presented with the condition under which each applies. Do not
  flatten them.
